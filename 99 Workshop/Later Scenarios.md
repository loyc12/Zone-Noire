## Post-Emergency Political Reckoning

The return to democracy should not create a simple good-versus-evil narrative.

The old regime:

- saved infrastructure
- preserved food systems
- maintained electricity
- prevented some regions from collapsing into uncontrolled violence

but also:

- used forced labour
- militarised borders
- committed serious abuses
- suppressed political opposition
- restricted movement
- operated lethal camp systems

Potential postwar issues:

- truth commissions
- criminal trials
- amnesties
- property restitution
- survivor compensation
- missing persons investigations
- archival disputes
- veterans' political organisations
- Hydro accountability
- camp memorialisation

There may be no clean national consensus.

## Québec in 2023

By 2023, Québec should probably be recognisably modern again.

It should **not** still look like a permanent wasteland.

Likely features:

- functioning democracy
- modern medicine partially or largely restored
- universities
- computers
- telecommunications
- advanced electrical systems
- regional trade
- industrial manufacturing
- functioning aviation, though smaller than in real history
- extensive rail

However, compared with real-world 2023 Québec:

- population is substantially smaller
- Montréal may be smaller or partially reconstructed
- Québec City is much more dominant
- private car ownership is lower
- passenger rail is more important
- energy consumption is heavily electrified
- Hydro-Québec is unusually powerful
- strategic reserves are politically important
- public civil-defence culture remains strong
- some southern exclusion zones remain
- material abundance is lower
- consumer culture is less dominant

## Québec City in 2023

Likely consequences of its survival:

- dominant political centre
- major administrative hub
- symbolic capital of survival
- expanded population
- greater economic role
- concentration of universities and national institutions
- centre of military and Hydro leadership

Its survival may reshape the traditional Montréal–Québec City relationship permanently.

## Social Memory

By 2023, the war occupies a role roughly analogous to a combination of:

- world war
- revolution
- national founding
- civil trauma
- famine

Likely remembered events include:

- the Day
- Québec City's survival
- the first winter
- the 1984 famine
- the border closure
- [[12 People/René Lévesque|Lévesque]]'s death
- creation of the camp system
- stabilisation
- reopening of major rail lines
- abolition of forced labour
- first restored elections

Different generations interpret these events differently.

## Later Generations

### Reconstruction generation

Born approximately 1995–2010.

Raised during improving material conditions.

May see emergency institutions as archaic.

### Post-restoration generation

Born after democratic normalisation.

Experiences the war mainly through family history, education, ruins, and memorial culture.

## Proposed Later Timeline

### 2000–2005

- compulsory labour curtailed
- camp closures begin
- municipal democracy expands
- emergency courts reduced
- military domestic role shrinks
- constitutional reform process begins

### 2005–2010

- broader elections return
- transitional constitutional settlement develops
- investigations into camp abuses begin
- Hydro's quasi-governmental powers are gradually separated from its utility role

### 2010–2015

- democratic constitutional order stabilises
- remaining emergency institutions are dismantled or normalised
- compensation, trials, memorialisation, and archival controversies dominate political debate

### 2015–2023

- Québec becomes a reconstructed industrial democracy
- rail and hydro remain disproportionately important
- Montréal's rebuilt status depends on later canon decisions
- exclusion zones and ruins remain
- emergency-era institutions retain powerful cultural memory
