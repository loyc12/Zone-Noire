# Post-Nuclear Québec — Context Handoff

## Purpose

This document is a **context handoff for bootstrapping a dedicated worldbuilding/documentation repository** for a post-nuclear alternate-history Québec setting.

It consolidates the currently remembered premise, later clarifications, and realism-oriented refinements discussed so far.

Treat all names, labels, institutional titles, periodizations, acronyms, and political terminology in this document as **provisional unless explicitly marked otherwise**. They should be reviewed before being promoted into project canon.

The intended tone of the setting is:

- grounded alternate history
- severe but plausible nuclear-war aftermath
- institutional and logistical realism
- political and moral ambiguity
- technological regression followed by uneven reconstruction
- strongly Québec-specific rather than generic post-apocalyptic fiction

The main inspiration is Cold War anti-nuclear media such as _Threads_, _The Day After_, _Testament_, and related realistic nuclear-war fiction, rather than _Fallout_, _Mad Max_, or other stylized wasteland settings.

---

# 1. Core Premise

The point of divergence occurs in **September 1983**, during the historical Soviet nuclear false-alarm incident.

In real history, the Soviet early-warning system incorrectly reported incoming American ICBMs. In this alternate timeline, the false alarm is not successfully dismissed, or is compounded by additional failures, causing escalation into a large-scale NATO–Warsaw Pact nuclear exchange.

The war effectively destroys the pre-existing North American political and economic system.

Major Canadian and American cities, military facilities, industrial centres, command infrastructure, transport hubs, and strategic targets are struck.

Québec suffers catastrophic damage, especially in the south.

Key premises currently assumed:

- Montréal is struck heavily and effectively ceases to function as a modern city
- Ottawa is destroyed or otherwise rendered incapable of acting as Canada's national government
- Much of southern Ontario and the northeastern United States is devastated
- Québec City is targeted, likely in connection with its political significance and nearby military infrastructure, but the relevant Soviet missile or warhead fails
- Québec City's survival later gains near-mythic significance
- The provincial government therefore survives in a situation where much of the federal government and continental infrastructure does not
- Canada and the United States cease to function coherently as centralized states during the immediate crisis
- Québec gradually becomes a de facto sovereign political unit through administrative necessity rather than necessarily through an immediate formal declaration of independence

The central setting concept is therefore not:

> "Québec survives nuclear war."

It is:

> **Enough of the Québec state survives to preserve organized society, but doing so progressively transforms that state into something authoritarian, coercive, highly centralized, and materially poorer.**

---

# 2. General Historical Arc

The intended broad progression is:

1. **1983 — Immediate survival**
    - nuclear exchange
    - government continuity
    - communications collapse
    - emergency requisitions
    - refugee flows
    - grid fragmentation
    - sanitation and medical collapse
2. **1984–1987 — Famine and coercive emergency rule**
    - severe climatic disruption
    - agricultural failure
    - mass mortality
    - border militarization
    - forced labour
    - expansion of rationing
    - consolidation of police and military authority
    - Hydro-Québec becomes an increasingly general reconstruction institution
3. **Late 1980s–1990s — Stabilization**
    - food security slowly improves
    - surviving infrastructure is consolidated
    - forced labour and camp systems become bureaucratically entrenched
    - the emergency government becomes effectively authoritarian
    - the fiction that normal prewar institutions will shortly return disappears
4. **Late 1990s–2000s — Reconstruction**
    - climatic conditions normalize
    - industry partially restarts
    - rail, hydroelectricity, sanitation, and machine-tool capacity expand
    - agricultural surplus returns
    - civilian economic activity recovers
    - pressure rises to end permanent emergency governance
5. **2000s–2010s — Democratic transition**
    - coercive labour systems are dismantled
    - municipal and then national elections return
    - military domestic powers are reduced
    - Hydro-Québec's political functions are curtailed
    - constitutional reconstruction occurs
    - past abuses become a major political issue
6. **2010s–2023 — Reconstructed postwar Québec**
    - Québec is again a functioning industrial society
    - it remains smaller, poorer, more electrified, more rail-oriented, and institutionally scarred compared with real-world Québec
    - the legacy of emergency rule remains central to political identity

# 3. The Nuclear Exchange

## 3.1 Immediate strategic consequences

The nuclear war should remain broadly realistic rather than over-specified unless a future target-map document establishes exact strikes.

Likely target categories include:

- Montréal
- Ottawa
- Toronto
- major northeastern American cities
- Canadian military bases
- airbases
- command-and-control facilities
- industrial hubs
- transportation chokepoints
- selected electrical infrastructure
- possibly Québec City and/or the Valcartier military complex


The exact strike list remains **OPEN**.

Important design principle:

> Nuclear destruction should be highly uneven.

Some areas are annihilated, some are contaminated, some suffer only indirect collapse.

Some retain considerable physical infrastructure but lose access to fuel, medicine, transportation, spare parts, and markets.

# 4. Québec City's Survival

Québec City's survival is foundational.

The city itself is targeted or endangered, but one or more intended Soviet warheads fail to function properly.

Possible mechanisms include:

- missile failure
- guidance failure
- warhead failure
- launch failure
- interception is less desirable unless independently justified
- target-assignment error

The exact mechanism remains **OPEN**.

The survival later acquires symbolic importance.

Possible later term:

- **"Le Miracle de Québec"** — PROVISIONAL


This should not necessarily imply a religious consensus.

It may function as:

- a popular phrase
- state propaganda
- dark humour
- patriotic mythology
- religious interpretation for some groups
- shorthand for the city's statistical luck


Québec City thereafter becomes the main surviving political and administrative centre of Québec.

# 5. Government Continuity

René Lévesque remains premier at the time of the war.

The provincial government initially attempts to preserve constitutional continuity.

Early assumptions may include:

- Canada still formally exists
- federal authority may eventually recover
- emergency measures are temporary
- Québec remains a province in legal theory
- federal assets are being "temporarily administered" rather than permanently seized

In practice, federal authority rapidly becomes irrelevant.

The Québec government progressively assumes control over:

- federal depots
- communications facilities
- military stores
- transport assets
- food stocks
- fuel stocks
- police coordination
- surviving federal personnel
- border control
- economic allocation


The transition to de facto sovereignty should be gradual and administrative rather than immediately ideological.

This creates room for legal ambiguity for several years.

---

# 6. René Lévesque and Political Legitimacy

Lévesque serves as the main surviving link to the prewar democratic order.

His importance is partly constitutional and partly symbolic.

During the first years:

- emergency decrees are still issued by an elected government
- the population remembers normal electoral politics
- old ministries and parliamentary institutions formally continue
- authoritarian measures remain framed as temporary emergency necessities


Lévesque dies several years after the war, likely around **1987**, close to the timing of his real-world death.

His death functions as a major symbolic turning point.

After his death:

- elections are postponed indefinitely
- succession occurs through emergency institutional mechanisms
- government legitimacy shifts from democratic continuity to survival performance
- coercive institutions become less constrained by prewar political norms


Possible historiographical distinction:

- **La Continuité** — pre-Lévesque-death emergency period
- **Le Gouvernement d'urgence** — later authoritarian period


Both terms are PROVISIONAL.

- logistics capacity
- telecommunications
- heavy equipment
- regional infrastructure
- hydroelectric generating assets
- technical management culture
- province-wide organizational reach


Hydro's mandate progressively expands into:

- electrical generation
- grid stabilization
- transmission repair
- telecommunications
- municipal electrical restoration
- water pumping
- sanitation infrastructure
- industrial energy allocation
- railway electrification
- heavy civil engineering
- machine-shop operations
- equipment salvage
- spare-parts fabrication
- dam maintenance
- strategic industrial planning


Over time Hydro effectively becomes part:

- utility
- reconstruction ministry
- civil engineering corps
- industrial conglomerate
- logistical institution
- emergency infrastructure command


Possible colloquial term:

- **"le Corps"** — PROVISIONAL


Possible more formal future titles remain OPEN.

Hydro eventually becomes one of the three major postwar power centres:

1. civilian political administration
2. military/security institutions
3. Hydro-Québec / technical reconstruction apparatus


Hydro accumulates extensive **soft power** rather than necessarily formally governing.

# 8. Electrical Grid Collapse and Recovery

The electrical system should not simply be destroyed by a single EMP event.

EMP may aggravate failures, but the larger collapse comes from:

- destroyed substations
- severed transmission corridors
- damaged control systems
- communications failure
- loss of skilled personnel
- rapid load disappearance
- instability from collapsing neighbouring grids
- transformer damage
- lack of replacement equipment
- sabotage, fires, or physical destruction


The most realistic response is grid **fragmentation and islanding**.

Hydro prioritizes:

- generating stations
- hospitals
- government facilities
- water and sewage
- radio
- machine shops
- food storage
- transport infrastructure
- strategic industries


Some communities remain outside reliable state power for years.

A useful social/geographical distinction develops between areas:

- **on the grid**
- **off the grid**


Possible project term:

- **"le Réseau"** — PROVISIONAL


Being connected to the restored grid also implies access to:

- communications
- public administration
- sanitation
- refrigeration
- workshops
- security
- medical services


The electrical map therefore increasingly overlaps with the effective political map.

# 9. Collapse of Healthcare and Sanitation

The healthcare crisis is not simply a shortage of doctors.

The support system of modern medicine disappears extremely quickly.

Critical shortages include:

- pharmaceuticals
- antibiotics
- insulin
- anaesthetics
- sterile supplies
- laboratory reagents
- blood products
- vaccines
- oxygen
- refrigeration
- disposable equipment
- replacement medical machinery


Hospitals simultaneously face:

- burns
- trauma
- radiation exposure
- displaced populations
- infectious disease
- sanitation failure
- malnutrition


Water and sewage systems are major vulnerabilities.

Likely crises include:

- contaminated drinking water
- sewage backup
- overloaded latrines
- dysentery
- respiratory epidemics
- lice
- tuberculosis
- wound infections
- dramatically increased maternal mortality
- dramatically increased infant mortality


Public-health officials, water engineers, chemists, and sanitation specialists become strategically important.

# 10. Food Crisis

Food becomes the main driver of political transformation.

The immediate war kills large numbers of people.

The subsequent food collapse kills many more.

The September timing creates a mixed situation:

- some crops have already matured
- harvesting is disrupted
- transport collapses
- fuel becomes scarce
- storage and refrigeration fail
- food-processing industries stop
- warehouses become strategic assets


The state rapidly requisitions:

- grain
- seed
- fertilizer
- livestock
- preserved food
- feedstocks
- farm machinery
- diesel
- distribution centres


Food rationing begins rapidly.

Private hoarding becomes a serious offence.

# 11. Nuclear Winter / Climatic Disruption

The postwar climate enters a severe multiyear disruption.

Exact global temperature figures should remain undefined unless future research establishes a specific war model.

Relevant consequences:

- colder summers
- abnormal frost
- reduced sunlight
- shorter growing seasons
- lower precipitation in some areas
- unpredictable rainfall
- reduced crop maturation
- several consecutive poor harvests


The first postwar summer, especially **1984**, may be remembered as the worst agricultural year.

Possible term:

- **"l'Été noir"** — PROVISIONAL


Possible broader term:

- **"le Long Hiver"** — PROVISIONAL


The climatic effects should diminish progressively over several years rather than remain permanently catastrophic.

# 12. Agriculture

Québec agriculture is especially vulnerable because of its relatively short growing season.

Emergency food policy shifts toward resilient caloric crops.

Likely emphasis:

- potatoes
- oats
- barley
- rye
- root vegetables
- cold-tolerant grains
- greenhouse crops where electricity is available


Livestock production declines sharply because feeding animals becomes inefficient during famine.

Large numbers of animals are slaughtered early.

Agricultural labour becomes highly regulated.

The government may consolidate farms or settlements to improve:

- security
- labour allocation
- machinery access
- food collection
- seed distribution
- rationing

# 13. Radioactive Contamination

Terminology note:

Food is primarily **radioactively contaminated**, not merely "irradiated".

Important radionuclide concerns include:

- iodine-131 in the immediate aftermath
- caesium-137 for long-term environmental contamination
- strontium-90 for long-term food-chain and skeletal exposure


Strontium-90 is especially politically important because it behaves chemically like calcium and can enter milk, bones, and teeth.

Contamination should be highly uneven.

Southern Québec may contain:

- severe fallout corridors
- relatively clean districts
- contaminated agricultural zones
- abandoned regions


This can create formal agricultural classifications.

Possible provisional classification:

- **Zone blanche** — monitored/acceptable
- **Zone grise** — restricted agriculture
- **Zone noire** — prohibited or heavily restricted settlement


These names are PROVISIONAL.

Future documentation should establish:

- strike map
- prevailing winds
- fallout corridors
- long-term exclusion areas
- resettlement policy

# 14. Refugee Crisis

The refugee crisis begins with internal displacement.

Early refugees include:

- Montréal residents
- southern Québec residents
- communities near contaminated targets
- people fleeing infrastructure collapse


The government initially responds using:

- schools
- arenas
- churches
- barracks
- municipal shelters
- temporary camps


The situation becomes qualitatively worse when refugees begin entering from:

- Ontario
- New England
- other northeastern American regions


The surviving Québec food system cannot support unrestricted immigration.

The southern frontier therefore becomes increasingly militarized.

Progression:

1. emergency checkpoints
2. controlled crossings
3. refugee screening
4. border closure
5. fortified crossing points
6. interdiction of unauthorized entry


This is one of the main drivers of postwar authoritarianism.

# 16. Forced Labour and Camp System

Refugee camps gradually transform into coercive labour institutions.

Original function:

- shelter
- registration
- food distribution
- health screening
- temporary resettlement


Later functions:

- labour allocation
- agricultural work
- forestry
- construction
- salvage
- railway repair
- industrial labour


Residents may be promised:

- permanent residency
- citizenship
- resettlement
- eventual repatriation
- family reunification
- labour credits


Possible concept:

- **Crédits d'établissement** ( Crédits ) — PROVISIONAL
- **Centre de Restoration Agricole** ( CDRA ) — PROVISIONAL


The system gradually becomes forced labour in practice.

Conditions are often miserable.

Mortality can result from:

- malnutrition
- exposure
- poor sanitation
- disease
- workplace accidents
- violence
- inadequate medical treatment
- punitive ration reductions


The system is not necessarily created as a deliberate extermination mechanism.

Its brutality emerges through the logic of extreme scarcity and bureaucratic coercion.

# 17. Ethnolinguistic Dimension

The camp and refugee system eventually gains a strong anglophone dimension.

This should arise structurally rather than from an immediately explicit anti-anglophone policy.

Reasons include:

- later refugee waves arrive from Ontario and the United States
- francophone Québec residents are more likely to possess local family networks
- citizens receive priority over foreigners
- anglophone newcomers have less political representation
- some southern anglophone Québec communities may also be displaced


Over time, anglophones become disproportionately represented among:

- camp labourers
- agricultural labourers
- resettlement populations
- border detainees
- industrial work units


This can evolve into deliberate discrimination later.

Possible political justifications may involve:

- food scarcity
- security
- citizenship
- cultural preservation
- loyalty concerns


The setting should treat this as a morally serious and historically contested institution rather than merely flavour.

# 21. Transportation

Transport becomes one of the main reconstruction bottlenecks.

Québec has abundant hydroelectric potential but lacks domestic petroleum production sufficient to preserve prewar mobility.

Petroleum constraints affect:

- tractors
- trucks
- aviation
- military vehicles
- construction equipment
- chemical production
- lubricants
- plastics
- mining
- transport


This strongly incentivizes electrification.

Likely priorities:

1. hydroelectric generation
2. transmission
3. rail
4. telecommunications
5. agriculture
6. heavy industry
7. civilian consumption


Railways become strategically central.

Likely developments:

- restoration of freight rail
- railway electrification
- expansion of passenger rail
- limited road maintenance
- rationed motor vehicles
- use of rivers and the St. Lawrence for transport
- revival of animal traction in rural areas

# 22. Technology Regression

The setting should avoid a clean return to the 19th century.

The more realistic model is **salvage industrialism** or **deindustrialized technical society**.

People retain:

- scientific knowledge
- books
- machine tools
- electrical motors
- radios
- vehicles
- industrial materials
- modern engineering concepts


They lose:

- global supply chains
- cheap replacement parts
- petrochemicals
- specialized electronics
- industrial-scale pharmaceuticals
- precision manufacturing
- abundant transport


A rural settlement in the 1990s might therefore contain:

- horse-drawn carts
- repaired 1970s tractors
- communal electrical lighting
- radio communications
- welded salvage equipment
- scarce chainsaws
- mechanical workshops
- almost no private fuel use


Technological development becomes highly uneven.

# 23. The North

Northern Québec requires dedicated development.

Important factors:

- James Bay hydroelectric installations
- distance from major strike targets
- difficulty of resupply
- infrastructure dependence
- Cree and Inuit political autonomy
- local knowledge
- northern transport


The catastrophe could increase the bargaining power of northern Indigenous governments.

Québec depends heavily on northern hydroelectric production.

However, the central government lacks the manpower to directly dominate enormous northern territories.

This could produce a postwar political settlement involving:

- Québec
- Cree governments
- Inuit governments
- northern Hydro administrations


Possible concept:

- a Québec–Cree–Inuit compact


No final structure has been decided.

This area should not be treated as empty northern hinterland.

# 28. Recovery

Recovery should eventually become substantial.

If Québec preserves:

- literacy
- organized government
- agricultural knowledge
- universities or technical schools
- hydroelectric power
- machine tools
- engineers
- basic industrial capacity


then full civilizational collapse should not remain permanent.

Important principle:

> Technical knowledge survives more easily than industrial capacity.

Once food production stabilizes, specialization can rapidly return.

Recovery loop:

food surplus
→ specialized labour
→ better maintenance
→ greater production
→ expanded industrial capacity
→ further surplus.

The most severe bottleneck is likely the first decade.

# 29. Democratic Restoration

Permanent dictatorship is not required.

A plausible transition:

## 1983–1987

Emergency powers exercised by a government still directly linked to prewar democratic legitimacy.

## 1987–mid-1990s

Consolidated authoritarian emergency government.

## Late 1990s

Civilian institutions begin regaining autonomy.

## 2000s

Municipal democracy returns.

## 2000s–2010s

National constitutional transition.

## 2010s

Functional democratic rule restored.

The exact timetable remains adjustable.

The key political question becomes:

> If the emergency regime exists to preserve civilization, what happens when civilization has visibly been preserved?

# 30. Post-Emergency Political Reckoning

The return to democracy should not create a simple good-versus-evil narrative.

The old regime:

- saved infrastructure
- preserved food systems
- maintained electricity
- prevented some regions from collapsing into uncontrolled violence


but also:

- used forced labour
- militarized borders
- committed serious abuses
- suppressed political opposition
- restricted movement
- operated lethal camp systems


Potential postwar issues:

- truth commissions
- criminal trials
- amnesties
- property restitution
- survivor compensation
- missing persons investigations
- archival disputes
- veterans' political organizations
- Hydro accountability
- camp memorialization


There may be no clean national consensus.

Likely features:

- functioning democracy
- modern medicine partially or largely restored
- universities
- computers
- telecommunications
- advanced electrical systems
- regional trade
- industrial manufacturing
- functioning aviation, though smaller than in real history
- extensive rail

- Montréal may be smaller or partially reconstructed
- Québec City is much more dominant

- energy consumption is heavily electrified
- Hydro-Québec is unusually powerful

- public civil-defence culture remains strong

- material abundance is lower
- consumer culture is less dominant

# 35. Generational Structure

Possible broad cohorts:

## Prewar generation

Remember normal 1970s–1980s life.

Often more politically conservative about emergency institutions because they directly experienced collapse.

## War children

Born shortly before or after 1983.

Grew up under famine, authoritarianism, rationing, and reconstruction.

Likely central to democratization.

## Reconstruction generation

Born approximately 1995–2010.

Raised during improving material conditions.

May see emergency institutions as archaic.

## Post-restoration generation

Born after democratic normalization.

Experiences the war mainly through family history, education, ruins, and memorial culture.

# 42. Important Realism Constraints

Future work should generally preserve the following principles.

## 42.1 Radiation is uneven

Avoid treating all of southern Québec as uniformly lethal.

## 42.2 EMP is not the sole explanation for grid collapse

Physical damage, grid instability, personnel losses, and spare-parts scarcity matter more.

## 42.3 Nuclear winter is severe but temporary

The climate shock may devastate several growing seasons without permanently freezing Québec.

## 42.4 Knowledge survives

Society loses industrial capability faster than scientific knowledge.

## 42.5 Recovery should accelerate once food security returns

Forty years is enough for substantial reconstruction if organized government persists.

## 42.6 Forced labour should develop through policy escalation

It is more credible if coercion evolves gradually from rationing and labour allocation.

## 42.7 Authoritarianism should remain institutionally understandable

The regime does not need to begin as ideologically fascist or totalitarian.

## 42.8 Democracy can plausibly return

A permanent dictatorship is not required if the underlying emergency eventually recedes.

## 42.9 Québec-specific institutions should matter

Avoid generic wasteland structures where real Québec institutions can plausibly evolve instead.

# 44. Canon Status Summary

## Relatively Stable Core Concepts

These appear central to the setting and should probably be preserved unless contradicted by future work:

- nuclear war begins in September 1983
- Montréal suffers major nuclear destruction
- Québec City survives an intended strike
- federal Canadian governance effectively collapses
- Québec's provincial state survives
- René Lévesque leads the initial emergency response
- Lévesque dies several years later
- his death symbolizes the end of effective prewar democratic continuity
- Hydro-Québec becomes a powerful reconstruction institution
- the grid is treated as critical to state survival
- healthcare and sanitation collapse
- severe postwar climatic disruption causes famine
- food scarcity drives increasingly authoritarian policy
- major refugee waves arrive from southern Québec, Ontario, and the northeastern United States
- the southern border becomes militarized
- forced labour camps emerge
- surviving military and police organizations are integrated into a Québec-controlled force
- petroleum scarcity pushes the economy toward electrification and rail
- rural society becomes materially poorer and partially deindustrialized
- organized technical knowledge survives

- authoritarian emergency rule does not necessarily remain permanent
- democracy plausibly returns during the 21st century

# 46. Guidance for the Dedicated Agent

When developing this setting:

- distinguish **canon**, **working assumptions**, and **proposals**
- do not silently resolve open questions
- flag contradictions with existing documentation
- prefer real 1983 Québec institutions over invented equivalents where plausible
- use historical Québec political, industrial, military, agricultural, and demographic data when useful
- keep nuclear effects grounded in plausible physics and logistics
- treat radiation, climate, famine, and infrastructure failure as distinct systems
- avoid assuming total technological amnesia
- avoid generic post-apocalyptic tropes where institutional degradation gives a stronger explanation
- treat authoritarian policies as historically contingent rather than inevitable
- preserve moral ambiguity around emergency institutions
- give Hydro-Québec, transport, food, sanitation, and technical labour unusually high narrative importance
- remember that the setting should become **progressively less post-apocalyptic and more post-post-apocalyptic** over four decades
- treat 2023 as a reconstructed society shaped by catastrophe, not a civilization still frozen in the immediate aftermath


When introducing terminology, institutional names, acronyms, or historical period labels:

> **Mark them as proposals unless they have been explicitly approved.**
